# Comic 005: The Guardrails Were The Road

Fictional industry scenario. People, organizations, workflows, and figures are illustrative.

## Status

Developed concept for production draft

## Core Message

The approved AI route with security checks and guardrails may look slower, but it is the route that can actually reach production safely.

## Core Critique

Enterprise teams often mistake security checks for delay while treating shiny unreviewed AI tools as progress. The failure is not that security slows the road down; the failure is skipping stability checks, then driving an untested vehicle on an unguarded cliff route.

## Vehicle Analogy

Security requirements are both vehicle inspection and road design. Tire checks, brake checks, inspection stickers, and stability review represent production readiness checks. Guardrails, lane shoulders, stop signs, monitoring cameras, and trip logs represent safe route design. Shiny AI vehicles on unguarded shortcuts may look faster, but untested wheels plus no guardrails create the real risk.

## Audience

Enterprise AI leaders, security teams, platform teams, pilot users, and product teams trying to balance speed with safe production adoption.

## Candidate Four-Panel Flow

1. Two teams choose routes. The practical team takes a regular, well-maintained AI car through the approved route. The fancy team takes a shiny advanced AI race car toward a cliffside shortcut.
2. The approved car gets a quick security and stability check: wheel bolts, brakes, inspection sticker, "NO PII," "Monitoring," and "Trip Log." Someone complains that the route feels slower.
3. The shiny race car speeds ahead on the unguarded shortcut. A sign says "Security Check Skipped," and one tire is visibly wobbling loose.
4. The practical car arrives at "Production Value." One driver asks where the fast team went. In the background, the race car's tire has flown off and the car is falling off the unguarded cliff, with a few papers and parts scattering.

## Image-Generation Notes

Use the "First-Principles Deadpan" direction: monochrome black-and-white line art, simple stick-figure people, and more detailed road infrastructure. The practical car should be modest, well-maintained, and calm, not pathetic. The advanced race car should look sleek enough to make the shortcut tempting, but the joke must come from skipped checks plus missing guardrails, not from mocking model capability itself.

Panel 2 should make the inspection visual crisp: wheel check, brake check, inspection sticker, "NO PII," "Monitoring," and "Trip Log." Panel 3 should show the skipped check turning into a visible wobbling tire. Panel 4 should use a wide enough view that the successful inspected route is foregrounded and the shortcut failure is obvious but controlled in the background: one shiny race car falls off the cliff after a tire flies off, with a few papers, a binder, and parts scattering. Keep it editorial and non-gory; the danger should be absurd and unmistakable, not chaotic.

Avoid making security look like a bureaucratic villain. The security checks should feel like ordinary production readiness and road infrastructure. Do not overload the comic with every possible control; use wheel/brake check, "NO PII," "Monitoring," and "Trip Log" as the main readable signals.

## Pre-Production Design Check

- Causal chain: the fast team skips security and stability checks; an unchecked tire comes loose; no guardrails turn the mechanical failure into a cliff failure.
- Visual contrast: ordinary inspected car on a guarded route versus shiny uninspected race car on an unguarded shortcut.
- Final consequence: the race car falls because the tire failure from Panel 3 meets the missing guardrails from Panel 1 and Panel 3.
- Clutter risk: keep the security checklist short; use one loose tire, a few papers, and a few parts instead of a full chaotic explosion.

## Open Questions
